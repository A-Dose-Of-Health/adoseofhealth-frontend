# Gynaecological Conditions & Cancers

## Introduction: What Are Gynaecological Conditions?

Gynaecological conditions affect the uterus, ovaries, fallopian tubes, cervix, vagina and vulva. They range from benign growths and hormonal disorders to infections and cancers. Many share overlapping symptoms — which is why proper diagnosis matters. Self-diagnosing based on a friend's experience can lead to years of unnecessary suffering.

- **190M** women worldwide with endometriosis
- **70–80%** of women develop fibroids by age 50
- **5,845** cervical cancer cases per year in Kenya

---

## Endometriosis

*Endometriosis is a condition where tissue similar to the uterine lining grows outside the uterus — on the ovaries, fallopian tubes, bowel, bladder, and other pelvic structures. This misplaced tissue responds to hormonal cycles: thickening, breaking down and bleeding with each period, but with no way to exit the body. This causes inflammation, scarring, adhesions and chronic pain. It is one of the leading causes of infertility.*

### Key Symptoms of Endometriosis

- **Severe, debilitating period pain** (dysmenorrhea) that OTC medication doesn't help
- **Chronic pelvic pain** even when not on your period
- **Pain during or after sex** (deep dyspareunia)
- Heavy menstrual bleeding or bleeding between periods
- Pain when urinating or having a bowel movement, especially during your period
- Fatigue
- **Difficulty conceiving (infertility)**
- Mood disorders and anxiety
- If in the chest: shortness of breath, coughing blood

---

### Diagnosis & The 4–12 Year Delay

The average time to diagnose endometriosis is **4–12 years** — because symptoms are often dismissed as "normal period pain."

**Investigations:**
- Pelvic exam — may feel cysts or scars behind the uterus
- Transvaginal ultrasound — can identify endometriomas ("chocolate cysts") on ovaries
- MRI — detailed images of deeper lesions
- **Laparoscopy** — minimally invasive surgery; the gold standard for definitive diagnosis. Allows direct visualisation and biopsy of lesions.

---

### Treatment Options for Endometriosis

There is no cure for endometriosis, but treatment can manage symptoms.

| Treatment | What It Does | Notes |
|-----------|------------|-------|
| **NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen)** | Reduce mild pain by lowering prostaglandins | First-line for pain management; often insufficient alone for severe endometriosis |
| **Combined contraceptive pill** | Suppresses the hormonal cycle that drives growth and bleeding of lesions | Must be stopped if trying to conceive — discuss timing with provider |
| **Mirena IUD (hormonal coil)** | Progestin locally thins the uterine lining; reduces pain and bleeding | Also provides contraception; fertility returns upon removal |
| **GnRH agonists** | Temporarily induce menopause by suppressing estrogen, shrinking lesions | Usually short-term (max 6 months) — can cause bone loss and menopausal symptoms |
| **Laparoscopic excision** | Surgical removal ("cutting out") of lesions — significant pain relief and improved fertility | Can be combined with treatment of endometriomas; may need repeat procedures |
| **IVF / Fertility treatment** | Assisted fertility methods if endometriosis affects conception | Discuss timing carefully — surgery may improve IVF outcomes in some cases |

## Uterine Fibroids (Leiomyomas)

*Fibroids are benign (non-cancerous) growths of smooth muscle and fibrous tissue that develop in or on the uterus. They range from tiny seedlings to large masses distorting the uterus. Around 2 in 3 women develop at least one fibroid. Many have no symptoms and don't know they have them — diagnosed by chance during a routine scan.*

### 4 Types by Location

- **Submucosal:** Grow just under the uterine lining, bulging into the cavity. Most likely to cause heavy bleeding and infertility.
- **Intramural:** Grow within the muscular wall of the uterus — the most common type.
- **Subserosal:** Grow on the outside of the uterus, projecting into the pelvis. Can cause pressure symptoms.
- **Pedunculated:** Subserosal or submucosal fibroids on a stalk — can occasionally twist (torsion), causing acute pain.

---

### Key Symptoms of Uterine Fibroids (only 1 in 3 women have symptoms)

- Heavy, prolonged menstrual bleeding — sometimes with large clots
- Pelvic pressure and pain (feeling of fullness or heaviness)
- Frequent urination (pressure on bladder)
- Constipation (pressure on bowel)
- Backache or leg pain
- Pain during sex
- Infertility or pregnancy complications (depending on location)

---

### Diagnosis Methods

| Test | What It Shows |
|------|-------------|
| **Pelvic exam** | May reveal enlarged, irregularly shaped uterus |
| **Ultrasound (transvaginal or abdominal)** | Primary imaging tool — identifies fibroids, maps location, measures size |
| **Hysteroscopy** | Camera through vagina and cervix into womb — best for submucosal fibroids. Takes 10–15 minutes; can feel like period cramping. |
| **Laparoscopy** | Camera through small abdominal incision; done under general anaesthesia — examines outside of uterus |
| **MRI** | Used for complex cases or detailed pre-surgical mapping |

---

### Treatment Options for Uterine Fibroids — Tailored to Your Goals

| Treatment | What It Does | Best For |
|-----------|------------|---------|
| **Watchful waiting** | Fibroids often shrink after menopause; monitor if asymptomatic | No symptoms or mild symptoms near menopause |
| **Mirena IUS** | Releases progestin locally — thins lining, reduces heavy bleeding | Heavy bleeding with smaller fibroids; also provides contraception |
| **Tranexamic acid** | Reduces blood loss by helping clots form — non-hormonal | Heavy bleeding; taken during period only |
| **GnRH agonists (injections)** | Temporarily shrink fibroids by stopping oestrogen — used before surgery | Usually max 6 months; causes menopausal symptoms; fibroids often regrow after stopping |
| **Myomectomy** | Surgical removal of fibroids, preserving the uterus | Women who still want children — fibroids can regrow. Done by keyhole or open surgery. |
| **Hysterectomy** | Complete removal of the uterus — permanently ends fibroids and heavy bleeding | Severe symptoms when family is complete. Subtotal (cervix kept) or total (cervix removed). |
| **Uterine Artery Embolisation (UAE)** | Blocks blood supply to fibroids via a catheter — causes them to shrink. Done under local anaesthetic; 1–2 week recovery. | Alternative to hysterectomy; pregnancy still possible but effects on fertility not fully understood |
| **Endometrial ablation** | Removes the lining of the womb — reduces heavy bleeding. ~20 minutes, day procedure. | Not for women wanting future pregnancy — significant risks if pregnancy occurs after |

## Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)

*PCOS is a hormonal disorder affecting how the ovaries work. It is one of the most common hormonal conditions in women of reproductive age — and it is both a reproductive and metabolic condition. It increases the long-term risk of type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and heart disease.*

### The Rotterdam Criteria — Diagnosis Needs 2 of 3

1. **Irregular or absent ovulation** (irregular periods)
2. **Clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism** (excess hair growth, acne, or elevated testosterone on blood tests)
3. **Polycystic ovaries on ultrasound** (12+ follicles in one ovary OR ovarian volume >10 mL)

*Note: Despite the name, you do not actually have "cysts" — the follicles are underdeveloped sacs in which eggs develop but cannot mature and release.*

---

### Key Symptoms of Polycystic Ovary Syndrome

- Irregular or absent periods (oligomenorrhea or amenorrhea)
- Difficulty conceiving (infertility due to lack of ovulation)
- Excess facial or body hair (hirsutism)
- Acne (especially lower face, chest, upper back)
- Weight gain or difficulty losing weight, especially around the abdomen
- Thinning hair on the scalp (male-pattern baldness)
- Darkening of skin in folds (acanthosis nigricans) — neck, armpits, groin

---

### Why Insulin Resistance Matters in PCOS

Many women with PCOS are resistant to insulin — their body produces extra amounts to compensate. High insulin stimulates the ovaries to produce more testosterone, disrupting normal ovulation and contributing to symptoms. Insulin resistance can also lead to weight gain, which further worsens the cycle by increasing insulin levels.

---

### Treatment Options for Polycystic Ovary Syndrome

| Goal | Treatment | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------|
| **First-line (all)** | Lifestyle modifications — as little as 5–10% weight loss can restore ovulation. Healthy diet, 150 min exercise/week, stress management, 6–8 hrs sleep. | Most effective foundation for ALL PCOS management |
| **Irregular periods** | Combined contraceptive pill or intermittent progestogen — regulates periods and reduces long-term endometrial cancer risk | Mirena IUS can also thin the lining without triggering regular bleeding |
| **Fertility** | Clomifene (first-line); letrozole; metformin (improves insulin resistance + ovulation); injectable gonadotrophins; IVF | Metformin is NOT a weight loss drug — it targets insulin resistance specifically |
| **Excess hair/acne** | Combined pill; eflornithine cream (facial hair); anti-androgens (spironolactone — not in pregnancy); laser removal | Anti-androgens require contraception as they can harm a male fetus |
| **Metabolic health** | GLP-1 agonists (Wegovy, Ozempic) for weight; cholesterol-lowering drugs; diabetes screening and management | Regular screening for diabetes and cardiovascular risk is essential |
| **Surgery (fertility only)** | Laparoscopic ovarian drilling (LOD) — minor procedure reducing androgen-producing tissue in ovaries to restore ovulation | Option when medications fail; lower multiple pregnancy risk than gonadotrophins |

## Adenomyosis

*Adenomyosis is a condition where the lining of the uterus (endometrium) grows into the muscular wall of the uterus (myometrium), causing the uterus to become enlarged, boggy and tender. It can occur alongside endometriosis and shares many overlapping symptoms. Historically diagnosed only after hysterectomy — today high-quality transvaginal ultrasound and MRI can diagnose it in living patients.*

### Symptoms of Adenomyosis

**Period-related:**
- Heavy or prolonged menstrual bleeding
- Severe cramping (sharp, stabbing pain during periods)

**Throughout the cycle:**
- Chronic pelvic pain
- Pain during sex
- Enlarged, tender uterus (felt on exam)
- Bloating or pressure in lower abdomen

*Some people with adenomyosis have no symptoms at all.*

---

### Diagnosis & Treatment

**Diagnosis:**
- Pelvic exam — enlarged, boggy, tender uterus
- Transvaginal ultrasound — shows characteristic thickening of uterine wall
- MRI — more detailed for complex cases

**Treatment:**
- NSAIDs and tranexamic acid for pain/bleeding
- Mirena IUD — excellent for reducing bleeding and pain
- Combined pill or GnRH agonists
- Endometrial ablation
- **Hysterectomy** — the only definitive cure; for severe cases when family is complete
- Iron supplements if anaemic from heavy bleeding

💡 **Key Diagnostic Clue**

If you have severe cramping and heavy bleeding, and an ultrasound shows an "enlarged uterus" but no fibroids — ask your provider: "Could this be adenomyosis?" It is frequently missed because it can appear as a vaguely enlarged uterus on routine scans.

## Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)

*PID is an infection of the female upper reproductive tract — the uterus, fallopian tubes and ovaries. Usually caused by STIs like chlamydia and gonorrhoea, but can also result from other vaginal bacteria ascending upward. Many people with PID have no symptoms or only mild symptoms — and don't realise they have an infection until they have trouble conceiving.*

### Symptoms of Pelvic Inflammatory Disease

- Lower abdominal pain (dull or cramping between lower tummy and thighs)
- Fever (sometimes)
- Unusual vaginal discharge (yellow/green, foul-smelling)
- Pain felt deep inside during sex
- Painful and/or heavy periods (unusual for you)
- Vaginal bleeding between periods or after sex

### Risk Factors

- Sexually active women under 25 are at highest risk
- Multiple partners or a partner with multiple partners
- Prior history of PID or STIs
- Having recently given birth, had a miscarriage, or had a termination
- Having had an IUD fitted, hysteroscopy or any procedure that opens the cervix

**Treatment:** Course of 2+ antibiotics covering multiple bacteria — often started immediately before test results return. Partner must also be treated to prevent reinfection.

---

### Complications of Untreated PID

| Complication | What It Means |
|-------------|--------------|
| Tubo-ovarian abscess | Pus-filled swellings in the fallopian tubes or ovaries — can be life-threatening |
| Chronic pelvic pain | Long-term pain from scarring and adhesions in the pelvis |
| Tubal factor infertility | Scarring and blocking of tubes prevents egg and sperm meeting — accounts for up to 1/3 of infertility cases |
| Ectopic pregnancy | Damaged tubes increase risk of pregnancy implanting in the tube instead of the uterus — a medical emergency |

⚠️ **Go to Hospital Immediately If:**

- Severe pelvic pain getting worse or hurting when you move or touch the area
- Pelvic pain with heavy vaginal bleeding
- Sudden, sharp intense pain in lower abdomen
- Pain under the ribs on the right side and in the right shoulder (may indicate spread)
- Feeling dizzy or faint

## Gynaecological Cancers — Overview

### FIGO Staging — Universal System for All Gynaecological Cancers

**Stage I:** Confined to the organ of origin. **Stage II:** Spread to nearby structures within the pelvis. **Stage III:** Spread to lymph nodes or other pelvic structures. **Stage IV:** Spread to distant organs (liver, lungs) or bladder/rectum. **Grade 1** = slow-growing; **Grade 3** = fast-growing. Together, stage and grade guide treatment and predict outcomes.

## Cervical Cancer — The One We Can Prevent

- **5,845** new cases per year in Kenya
- **3,286** deaths per year in Kenya
- **14** Kenyan women die from cervical cancer daily

Nearly all cervical cancers are caused by persistent **high-risk HPV** — a common virus transmitted through skin-to-skin sexual contact.

**Early stages:** NO symptoms — which is why screening is essential.

**As cancer develops:**
- Abnormal vaginal bleeding (after sex, between periods, after menopause)
- Watery or bloody vaginal discharge with foul odour
- Pelvic pain or pain during intercourse
- Pain when urinating

### Prevention — 90-70-90 Targets

Kenya's National Cervical Cancer Elimination Action Plan 2026–2030 targets:
- **90%** of girls fully vaccinated against HPV by age 15
- **70%** of women screened using high-performance tests (HPV DNA) by ages 35 and 45
- **90%** of women diagnosed receiving timely treatment

**HPV vaccine:** Free at age 10 at public health facilities; single-dose (since November 2025); protects against HPV types causing 90% of cervical cancers plus 5 other cancers.

🇰🇪 **Kenyan Science Behind Single-Dose HPV Vaccine**

The research establishing single-dose HPV vaccine efficacy — now adopted in 88 countries worldwide — was led by Kenyan women scientists: **Dr. Nelly Mugo, Dr. Elizabeth Bukusi, Dr. Maricianah Onono and Dr. Betty Njoroge** through the KENSHE study by KEMRI in collaboration with the University of Washington. Kenyan women scientists changed global vaccination policy.

## Uterine (Endometrial) Cancer

Most common in postmenopausal women (average age at diagnosis: 63). Rates rising because of increased life expectancy, changing reproductive trends, rising obesity, and lack of awareness.

**Risk factors:** obesity (BMI ≥31), unopposed estrogen (no progesterone), high blood pressure, Lynch syndrome, diabetes, late menopause (>55 years), tamoxifen use.

**The early warning sign:** Abnormal bleeding — any postmenopausal bleeding (even spotting), heavy or prolonged bleeding in premenopausal women, or watery blood-tinged discharge.

**Up to 80% of endometrial cancers could be prevented** if perimenopausal women with abnormal bleeding receive early intervention.

**Over 60%** of uterine cancer patients in Kenya are diagnosed at advanced stages (III or IV). Don't wait — if you have any bleeding after menopause, **see a provider immediately**.

---

## Ovarian Cancer — Recognising the Subtle Signs

Often called a "silent killer" because early symptoms are vague — but the symptoms ARE present, they are just subtle and easily dismissed. ~80% of patients are diagnosed at advanced stage.

**Kenya stats:** ~1,245 new cases/year; ~895 deaths/year.

**Risk factors:** Age >50; BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations (25% of cases); family history of breast/ovarian/colorectal cancer; endometriosis; nulliparity (never given birth); obesity.

### Symptoms of Ovarian Cancer — Persistent for >2 Weeks

If these symptoms are **new, persistent (lasting more than 2 weeks)** and different from what you normally experience:

- Bloating or increased abdominal size
- Pelvic or abdominal pain/pressure
- Difficulty eating or feeling full quickly (early satiety)
- Urinary urgency or frequency
- Fatigue, back pain, changes in bowel habits

*If BRCA mutation or strong family history: consider genetic counselling and discuss screening with your provider.*

---

## Vulvar Cancer — Highly Curable When Caught Early

Cancer beginning in the vulva (labia, clitoris, vaginal opening). Accounts for 5–8% of gynaecological malignancies. Kenya: ~200–250 cases/year; ~120 deaths/year. Median age: 68–70. Most are linked to HPV or chronic inflammation (lichen sclerosus). **Look at your vulva regularly** — because it's in a visible location, early detection is possible.

| Symptom | Action Required |
|---------|----------------|
| A persistent lump or mass on the vulva (most common presentation) | See a provider — requires biopsy to confirm diagnosis |
| Persistent itching that doesn't go away | Do not ignore — persistent pruritus always needs evaluation |
| Non-healing ulcer or sore on the vulva | Any sore that hasn't healed in 2–3 weeks must be evaluated |
| Skin changes: thickening, colour change (red, white, darker) | Especially if you have a history of lichen sclerosus — seek prompt review |
| Bleeding not related to periods | Requires immediate evaluation — do not wait |

## Treatment & Support in Kenya

### Where to Seek Cancer Care

- **Kenyatta National Hospital (KNH)** — National referral; comprehensive cancer care
- **Moi Teaching & Referral Hospital (MTRH)** — Comprehensive cancer care
- **Nakuru Regional Cancer Centre** — Regional care
- **Aga Khan University Hospital, MP Shah Hospital, Nairobi Hospital, Texas Cancer Hospital** — Private sector

### Health Financing

The **Social Health Authority (SHA)** has replaced NHIF. The cancer benefit package has changed from KSh 600,000/individual (NHIF) to KSh 400,000/household (SHA). The **Kenya Network of Cancer Organisations (KENCO)** has petitioned Parliament to enhance oncology benefits. Limited radiotherapy capacity means patients may wait months or travel long distances.

🚨 **When to Seek Urgent Care**

**Seek urgent care for:** Any bleeding after menopause (even spotting) · Bleeding between periods or after sex · A sore or lump on the vulva that does not heal · Persistent bloating, pelvic pain or feeling full quickly for >2 weeks · Unexplained weight loss · Changes in bowel or bladder habits

**Emergency care immediately for:** Sudden, severe pelvic pain · Heavy bleeding with faintness or dizziness · Fever with pelvic pain
