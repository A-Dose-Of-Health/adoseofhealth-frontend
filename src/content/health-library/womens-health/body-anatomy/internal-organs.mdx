## 1. What Is the Vagina?
The vagina is a stretchy, fibromuscular canal that connects the cervix (the lower part of the uterus) to the outside of the body. Think of it as a dynamic, self-cleaning tunnel designed for menstruation, intimacy and childbirth.
Many people use the word “vagina” to refer to all female genitals, but medically, the vagina is just one internal organ. The external genital organs are called the vulva.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Key Point">
The vagina is internal. The vulva is external. They are not the same. 
</Callout>
<Callout variant="info" title="Good to Know">
There is no single normal vagina. Vaginas vary in size, shape, depth, colour and the amount and type of discharge. Variation does not mean abnormality.
</Callout>
<Callout variant="warning" title="Important">
Abnormal bleeding, foul-smelling discharge, persistent itching or pain are not normal and should be evaluated.
</Callout>
<Callout variant="neutral" title="Taking The First Step">
If something feels different or unusual, speak to a healthcare provider. There is no shame in discussing vaginal health, it is routine healthcare.
</Callout>

## 2. Functions
The vagina plays several important roles:
<MdxList variant="glossary" title="Functions of the Vagina">
  <MdxTerm term="Menstruation">Menstrual blood exits the body through the vagina. Tampons, menstrual cups and discs are inserted here.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Conception">During penetrative sex, sperm enters the vagina and travels through the cervix, uterus and fallopian tubes toward an egg. The vaginal environment must be healthy and pH-balanced to support sperm survival.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Childbirth">During vaginal delivery, the baby passes through the vagina. This is why it is sometimes called the “birth canal.” The walls are designed to stretch dramatically and then return to their original state.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Sexual Function">The vaginal walls contain nerve endings that respond to arousal. When aroused:<br /> - Blood flow increases.<br /> - Lubrication (transudate) seeps through the walls.<br /> - The vagina expands and lengthens (tenting effect)</MdxTerm>
</MdxList>

<Callout variant="tip" title="Key Point">
The vagina is dynamic; it changes in size, shape and lubrication depending on arousal, hormones and life stage. 
</Callout>
<Callout variant="info" title="Good to Know">
Oestrogen strongly influences vaginal health. During menopause, lower oestrogen levels can cause dryness and thinning of the walls (vaginal atrophy). During breastfeeding, similar temporary changes can occur.
</Callout>
<Callout variant="warning" title="Important">
Pain during intercourse (dyspareunia) is not something you “just tolerate.” It should be evaluated.
</Callout>
<Callout variant="danger" title="About Medication">
Vaginal dryness in menopause may be treated with topical oestrogen or lubricants prescribed by a provider. Do not self-prescribe hormone therapy without medical guidance.
</Callout>
<Callout variant="neutral" title="Taking The First Step">
If intercourse is painful, lubrication is reduced, or bleeding occurs after sex, book a gynaecological evaluation.
</Callout>

## 3. Anatomy
The Vagina
<MdxList title="is located in the pelvis:">
  <MdxItem variant="bullet">In front of the rectum (back passage)</MdxItem>
  <MdxItem variant="bullet">Behind the bladder (where urine is stored)</MdxItem>
</MdxList>
<MdxList title="It connects:">
  <MdxItem variant="bullet">At the top → to the cervix and uterus</MdxItem>
  <MdxItem variant="bullet">At the bottom → to the vaginal opening (part of the vulva)</MdxItem>
</MdxList>

### The Three Layers of the Vaginal Wall
The vaginal wall is composed of three distinct layers:
<MdxList variant="glossary" title="Three main layers & their Functions">
  <MdxTerm term="Mucosal Layer (Inner Lining)">Similar to skin but without glands. Produces moisture via transudation (fluid seeping through walls). Contains rugae, transverse folds like pleats that allow dramatic stretching during sex and childbirth. Hosts Lactobacillus bacteria that produce lactic acid maintaining protective pH 3.8–4.5.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Muscular Layer (Middle Layer)">Smooth muscle that contracts and expands. Helps expel discharge and menstrual fluid. Surrounded and supported by the pelvic floor muscles — pelvic floor health directly affects vaginal function.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Adventitia Layer (Outer Layer)">Connective tissue rich in collagen and elastic fibres. Anchors the vagina to surrounding pelvic structures (bladder, rectum, urethra). Allows expansion and recoil. The slightly acidic discharge that can bleach dark underwear is completely normal — it's the vaginal flora doing their job.</MdxTerm>
</MdxList>
<Callout variant="tip" title="Key Point">
The vaginal flora; the healthy bacteria that naturally live in the vagina, are your first line of defence, helping maintain an acidic pH that prevents harmful bacteria and yeast from overgrowing. This natural acidity is also why black underwear may appear slightly bleached over time, as normal vaginal discharge is mildly acidic. It’s completely normal, so there’s no need to feel embarrassed, your body is simply doing what it was designed to do. 
</Callout>
<Callout variant="info" title="Good to Know">
The rugae are often compared to the pleats of a skirt. They allow the vagina to unfold and expand as needed, whether for a tampon, a baby or during arousal.
</Callout>

### The Vaginal Opening (Introitus)
The external entrance to the vaginal canal.
<MdxList title="It is located:">
  <MdxItem variant="bullet">Below the urethra (urine opening)</MdxItem>
  <MdxItem variant="bullet">Above the anus</MdxItem>
  <MdxItem variant="bullet">Surrounded by the labia minora (inner lips)</MdxItem>
</MdxList>

### The Hymen
A thin, membranous rim of tissue that surrounds or partially covers the vaginal opening. It is NOT a seal.
<MdxList variant="glossary" title="The Hymen">
  <MdxTerm term="Variation">Hymens vary widely. Some are a thin crescent moon shape, some have one or more openings, some are fringed and some are very small. A small percentage of people are born without one.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Stretching">The hymen can stretch or tear from many activities, including exercise, tampon use or medical exams. It is not a reliable indicator of sexual history or virginity.</MdxTerm>
</MdxList>

<Callout variant="tip" title="Key Point">
The hymen does not determine sexual history. It is a normal anatomical structure, not a "proof" of virginity. 
</Callout>
<Callout variant="info" title="Good to Know">
For some, the first experience of penetrative sex may cause the hymen to stretch, which can cause slight discomfort or a small amount of bleeding. For many others, there is no bleeding at all. Both are normal.
</Callout>

### Bartholin's Glands
Also called the greater vestibular glands, are two small, pea-sized glands located slightly inside the vaginal opening, at approximately the 4 o'clock and 8 o'clock positions.

<MdxList title="Function:">
  <MdxItem variant="bullet">Their primary function is to secrete a small amount of fluid during sexual arousal to provide lubrication for the vaginal opening.</MdxItem>
</MdxList>

<Callout variant="tip" title="Key Point">
You usually don't notice your Bartholin's glands when they are healthy. They only become noticeable if the duct becomes blocked or infected. 
</Callout>
<Callout variant="info" title="Good to Know">
These glands are not active until puberty. Before then, they are small and non-functional.
</Callout>

## 4. Common Conditions and Symptoms (Infections)
Because the vagina is a dynamic environment, it is susceptible to imbalances and infections. Here are the most common ones, explained.

### Vaginitis (Inflammation of the Vagina)
This is the most common vaginal condition. It refers to inflammation caused by an imbalance or infection. The three main types are:

<MdxList variant="glossary" title="Bacterial Vaginosis (BV)">
  <MdxTerm term="What It Is">An overgrowth of harmful bacteria, leading to a decrease in healthy Lactobacilli. It is the most common cause of vaginal discharge in reproductive-age women.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Symptoms">Thin, greyish or white discharge; a strong fishy odour (especially after sex); mild itching or burning.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Risk Factors">Douching, new or multiple sex partners, not using condoms. (BV is NOT an STI, but sexual activity can trigger it).</MdxTerm>
</MdxList>

<MdxList variant="glossary" title="Yeast Infection (Candidiasis)">
  <MdxTerm term="What It Is">An overgrowth of Candida (a fungus), most commonly Candida albicans.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Symptoms">Thick, white, cottage-cheese-like discharge; intense itching; redness and swelling of the vulva; burning during urination or sex.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Risk Factors">Antibiotic use (which kills healthy bacteria), high oestrogen levels (pregnancy, hormone therapy), uncontrolled diabetes, a weakened immune system such as in HIV/AIDS.</MdxTerm>
</MdxList>

<MdxList variant="glossary" title="Trichomoniasis (Trich)">
  <MdxTerm term="What It Is">A sexually transmitted infection caused by a parasite (Trichomonas vaginalis).</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Symptoms">Frothy, yellow-green discharge with a strong odour; itching and redness; pain during urination or sex; discomfort in the lower abdomen.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Risk Factors">Many people with trichomoniasis have no symptoms, so it can go undetected..</MdxTerm>
</MdxList>

### Other Vaginal Conditions

<MdxList variant="glossary" title="Vaginal Atrophy (Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause)">
  <MdxTerm term="What It Is">Thinning, drying, and inflammation of the vaginal walls due to decreased oestrogen, most commonly during menopause.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Symptoms">Vaginal dryness, burning, decreased lubrication, pain during sex and urinary urgency.</MdxTerm>
</MdxList>

<MdxList variant="glossary" title="Vaginitis (from Irritants)">
  <MdxTerm term="What It Is">Inflammation caused by an allergic reaction or irritation from products.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Common Culprits">Scented soaps, bubble baths, laundry detergents, fabric softeners, spermicides and douches.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Symptoms">Itching, burning, redness, but no abnormal discharge (unless there's also an infection).</MdxTerm>
</MdxList>

<MdxList variant="glossary" title="Vaginal Tears">
  <MdxTerm term="What They Are">Small cuts or fissures in the vaginal tissue, often caused by insufficient lubrication during sex or trauma during childbirth.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Symptoms">Sharp pain, stinging or visible small cuts.</MdxTerm>
</MdxList>

<Callout variant="tip" title="Key Point">
Vaginal symptoms often overlap. A yeast infection, BV and trichomoniasis can all cause itching and discharge, but they require completely different treatments. Self-diagnosis is unreliable. 
</Callout>
<Callout variant="warning" title="Important">
Don’t use over-the-counter antifungal creams if you aren't sure it's a yeast infection. Using them for BV or trichomoniasis will not work and can delay proper treatment.
</Callout>
<Callout variant="neutral" title="Taking The First Step">
If you notice a change in discharge, odour or comfort, book a clinic visit. A simple swab test can tell you exactly what is happening, so you can get the right treatment.
</Callout>

### Tests and Diagnosis
<MdxList variant="glossary" title="A healthcare provider may recommend one or more of the following to assess vaginal health:">
  <MdxTerm term="Pelvic Exam">A visual and manual inspection of the vagina and cervix.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Vaginal pH Test">A simple swab to check acidity. A high pH (above 4.5) can indicate BV or trichomoniasis.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Wet Mount (Microscopy)">A sample of discharge is mixed with a saline solution and examined under a microscope to look for trichomonads, clue cells (BV) or yeast.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Whiff Test">A drop of potassium hydroxide (KOH) is added to the discharge sample. A fishy odour suggests BV.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="STI Testing">A swab or urine test to check for chlamydia, gonorrhoea and trichomoniasis.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Fungal Culture">If a yeast infection is suspected but not visible on the wet mount, a culture can identify the specific type of fungus.</MdxTerm>
  <MdxTerm term="Colposcopy">If there are concerning areas on the vaginal walls, a magnifying device may be used to look more closely.</MdxTerm>
</MdxList>

<Callout variant="tip" title="Key Point">
Regular pelvic exams detect issues early, even before symptoms appear.
</Callout>

### How to Keep Your Vagina Healthy
The vagina is remarkably good at taking care of itself. Your job is to support it, not interfere.

<MdxList title="Daily Care">
  <MdxItem variant="check">Do not douche: The vagina is self-cleaning. Douching disrupts the healthy flora and pH, increasing the risk of BV, yeast infections and pelvic inflammatory disease.</MdxItem>
  <MdxItem variant="check">Clean gently: Wash only the external vulva with warm water. You do not need to wash inside the vagina.</MdxItem>
  <MdxItem variant="check">Wear breathable underwear: Cotton allows air circulation, keeping the area dry.</MdxItem>
  <MdxItem variant="check">Change promptly: Remove wet or sweaty clothing (swimsuits, gym wear) as soon as possible.</MdxItem>
  <MdxItem variant="check">Wipe correctly: Always wipe from front to back after using the toilet to prevent bacteria from the anus entering the vagina or urethra.</MdxItem>
  <MdxItem variant="check">Safe sex: Using condoms reduces the risk of STIs and helps maintain the vaginal pH balance.</MdxItem>
  <MdxItem variant="check">Stay hydrated: Good hydration supports overall mucous membrane health, including the vagina.</MdxItem>
</MdxList>

<MdxList title="What to Avoid">
  <MdxItem variant="cross">Scented tampons, pads, or wipes.</MdxItem>
  <MdxItem variant="cross">Harsh soaps, bubble baths or feminine sprays.</MdxItem>
  <MdxItem variant="cross">Prolonged wear of damp underwear.</MdxItem>
  <MdxItem variant="cross">Unprotected sex with new or untested partners.</MdxItem>
</MdxList>

## 5. Myth-Busting: Vaginal Health

| ❌ Myth | ✅ Truth |
|--------|---------|
| "The vagina must be washed inside to stay clean" | The vagina is self-cleaning. Its discharge and healthy bacteria keep it balanced. Internal washing (douching) removes good bacteria and increases infection risk. |
| "If there's no smell, there's no infection" | Many infections including chlamydia, gonorrhoea and early trichomoniasis often have NO symptoms. You cannot rely on smell or appearance alone. |
| "Pain during sex is normal for women" | Persistent pain during intercourse (dyspareunia) is a medical symptom, not a normal part of being a woman. It requires evaluation. |
| "Vaginal steaming cleanses the womb" | No evidence supports this. Steaming can cause serious burns to vaginal walls and disrupt healthy flora, leading to infections. |
| "The hymen determines virginity" | The hymen is a stretchy membrane with natural variation. It can be stretched by many non-sexual activities. It does not prove sexual history. |
| "Public toilets can give you an STI" | Most STI pathogens are fragile and cannot survive on toilet seats. They require close skin-to-skin or fluid contact for transmission. |

# The Cervix — The Gatekeeper

*The cervix is the lower, cylindrical part of the uterus that connects the uterine body to the vagina. Approximately 4 cm long and 3 cm in diameter. It is not a separate organ — it's an integral part of the uterus, acting as passageway, protective barrier and producer of critical fluids.*

---

## Four Critical Functions

**1. Gatekeeper for sperm:** Thick plug-like mucus most of the month; around ovulation becomes thin, clear and stretchy (egg-white) creating a sperm-friendly environment.

**2. Protective barrier:** Remains tightly closed during pregnancy.

**3. Childbirth pathway:** Softens, effaces and dilates to ~10 cm during labour.

**4. Menstrual passage:** Allows menstrual blood to exit.

---

## The Transformation Zone — Why It Matters

The cervix has two cell types: **Glandular cells** (columnar, line the canal, produce mucus — reddish velvet appearance) and **Squamous cells** (flat, protective, cover the ectocervix — smooth pink). Where they meet is the **transformation zone (squamocolumnar junction)** — the most common site for cervical cancer to develop. This is why Pap smears target this area specifically. Its location shifts with age and hormones.

✨ **Cervix Changes You Can Track**

The cervix changes texture and position throughout your menstrual cycle. Around ovulation it becomes **softer, higher and more open** (like pursed lips) to allow sperm entry. After ovulation it becomes **firmer, lower and closed**. Tracking these changes is a valid form of fertility awareness.

---

## Cervical Screening — Kenya Guidelines

| Test | What It Detects | When & How Often |
|------|----------------|-----------------|
| **Pap Smear** | Abnormal cell changes on the cervix that could potentially become cancer | Ages 21–25: every 3 years; Ages 25–65: every 3 years (preferred) or co-test with HPV every 5 years |
| **HPV Test** | High-risk HPV types (especially 16 & 18) that cause cell changes | Ages 26–65: every 5 years (preferred method); can be self-collected |
| **Co-testing** | Both HPV + Pap together | Every 5 years, ages 25–65 |
| **HIV-positive women** | Pap smear every year from age 21 | Annual — HIV increases cervical cancer risk significantly |

🇰🇪 **Kenya Context**

Kenya transitioned to a **single-dose HPV vaccination schedule in November 2025**, making it easier to reach more girls. The HPV vaccine is free of charge at public health facilities for girls aged 10 years. Research by Kenyan doctors — Dr. Nelly Mugo, Dr. Elizabeth Bukusi, Dr. Maricianah Onono and Dr. Betty Njoroge through the KENSHE study (KEMRI/University of Washington) — was instrumental in establishing single-dose efficacy globally.

# Uterus, Fallopian Tubes & Ovaries

---

## The Uterus (Womb)

A hollow, pear-shaped muscular organ — the central anatomical landmark of the female internal genitalia. Before pregnancy it is ~7–8 cm long. During pregnancy it expands to the ribcage and can weigh up to 1 kg. After menopause it atrophies and shrinks back down. About 80% of uteri are **"anteverted"** (tilted forward toward the bladder); others are **"retroverted"** (tilted backward) — both are normal variations, not problems.

### Three Layers of the Uterine Wall

**Perimetrium:** Thin outer serous membrane covering the uterus.

**Myometrium:** The thick middle muscular layer — powerfully contracts during menstruation and labour. Where fibroids grow.

**Endometrium:** The inner mucosal layer with two sublayers — the permanent *basal layer* and the *functional layer* that thickens and sheds monthly.

### Four Parts of the Uterus

**Fundus:** Broad, curved top above the fallopian tube openings.

**Body (Corpus):** Main central portion where most of the organ is.

**Isthmus:** Narrowed lower neck just above the cervix.

**Cervix:** The lower cylindrical part connecting to the vagina (covered in detail on the previous page).

Held in place by broad, round and uterosacral ligaments.

### Three Key Functions

**1. Menstruation:** Each month, the endometrium thickens in preparation for potential pregnancy; sheds if no fertilisation occurs.

**2. Implantation & fetal development:** A fertilised egg implants into the nutrient-rich endometrium; the uterus becomes the fetus's home throughout pregnancy.

**3. Labour & delivery:** Myometrial contractions dilate the cervix and push the baby through the birth canal.

---

## The Fallopian Tubes — Where Fertilisation Happens

Two narrow, muscular canals (~11–12 cm long) connecting the ovaries to the uterus. The tubes are *not* directly attached to the ovaries — they have finger-like projections called **fimbriae** that reach out to sweep the egg into the tube at ovulation. The tubes are alive with movement: cilia create a current and muscular walls contract to transport egg, sperm and embryo.

### Four Regions of Each Tube

**Intramural:** The shortest, narrowest section passing through the uterine wall.

**Isthmus:** Narrow segment next to the uterus.

**Ampulla:** The longest, widest part — most common site of fertilisation. Has folded lining for increased surface area.

**Infundibulum:** The funnel-shaped open end near the ovary, fringed with fimbriae.

### Tubal Conditions

**Salpingitis/PID:** Infection (usually chlamydia/gonorrhoea) causing inflammation, often with no symptoms. Can scar and block tubes, causing infertility.

**Hydrosalpinx:** Fluid-filled blocked tube — often from prior infection; causes infertility.

**Ectopic Pregnancy:** Fertilised egg implants in the tube instead of the uterus — a **medical emergency**. Symptoms: sudden sharp one-sided pain, vaginal bleeding, shoulder tip pain, dizziness. Requires immediate care.

**Tubal factor infertility:** Accounts for up to one-third of infertility cases.

---

## The Ovaries — Hormonal Architects

Two small, oval-shaped organs (~3.5 × 2 × 1 cm in reproductive years; shrink after menopause) — the female equivalent of the testes. They have two essential roles: producing eggs and secreting hormones.

### Egg Production — A Finite Resource

You are born with all the eggs you will ever have: ~6–7 million in utero, ~1–2 million at birth, ~300,000 at puberty. Only 300–400 will ever be ovulated. The rest naturally degenerate through **atresia**. You cannot make new eggs. This is why age-related fertility decline is real — both egg quantity and quality decrease over time.

### Hormones the Ovaries Produce

**Estrogen:** Develops secondary sex characteristics, thickens the uterine lining, maintains bone density, skin health, mood.

**Progesterone:** Prepares the uterine lining for implantation; dominant in the second half of the cycle; maintains early pregnancy.

**Testosterone:** Yes — women produce testosterone in small amounts; plays roles in libido, mood and muscle mass.

**Inhibin:** Regulates FSH production in the pituitary.

⚠️ **Seek Urgent Care If:**

Sudden, severe, one-sided pelvic pain could indicate **ovarian torsion** (twisting of the ovary on its ligaments, cutting off blood supply) or a **ruptured ovarian cyst** — both are medical emergencies. Pain accompanied by fever, nausea/vomiting or faintness requires immediate attention. Heavy bleeding with pain, or bleeding combined with a positive pregnancy test, also warrant urgent evaluation.

# The Urethra & Urinary Health

*The urethra is a narrow tube (~4 cm long) connecting the urinary bladder to the outside. Its primary job is urine elimination — it is NOT part of the reproductive system. However, because of its close proximity to the vagina and anus, it is deeply connected to pelvic health.*

---

## Structure: Three Layers

**Mucosal layer (inner):** Lined with transitional epithelium near the bladder, squamous near the opening. Contains folds allowing stretching.

**Spongy layer (submucosa):** Rich in blood vessels and elastic fibres; helps seal the urethra closed when not in use.

**Muscular layer (outer):** Smooth muscle fibres plus the external sphincter (voluntary control).

## Two Sphincters Control Urination

**Internal urethral sphincter:** Involuntary — opens automatically when the bladder is full.

**External urethral sphincter:** Voluntary — you control this to hold urine or let it out. Supported by the pelvic floor muscles.

The sensation "I need to pee" is triggered by **stretch receptors in the bladder wall**, not the urethra, when the bladder is about half full.

---

## UTI — Urinary Tract Infection

A UTI is **not an STI** — it is a completely separate system. Most commonly caused by E. coli from the digestive tract entering the urethra. The short female urethra (~4 cm vs 20 cm in males) is why women are far more prone.

| Symptom | Risk Factor | Prevention |
|---------|------------|-----------|
| Pain or burning during urination (dysuria) | Wiping back to front (brings bacteria forward) | **Always wipe front to back** — single most important hygiene habit |
| Frequent urge to urinate, passing only small amounts | Holding urine for too long | Urinate when you feel the urge; don't hold it |
| Cloudy, dark or bloody urine | Sexual activity ("honeymoon cystitis" — bacteria pushed into urethra during intercourse) | **Urinate after sex** — one of the most effective prevention strategies |
| Strong-smelling urine | Menopause (low oestrogen thins urethral tissues, reducing defences) | Stay hydrated — dilutes urine and flushes bacteria |
| Pelvic pressure or incomplete emptying | Spermicides or diaphragms | Wear breathable cotton underwear; avoid tight synthetics |

🚨 **Seek Urgent Care — Signs the Infection Has Reached the Kidneys**

**Fever, chills or shaking · Nausea or vomiting · Pain in your back or side (flank pain, just below ribs) · Inability to urinate · Visibly bloody urine (red or pink)**

These indicate **pyelonephritis** (kidney infection) — a more serious condition requiring immediate treatment. A simple lower UTI often has NO fever — do not wait for fever to seek care.

---

## Kenya Myth-Busting: Urinary Health

| ❌ Myth | ✅ Truth |
|--------|---------|
| "Drinking less water will reduce the burning" | Burning is caused by inflammation, not water volume. Drinking less concentrates urine, making burning worse. Drinking more helps dilute and flush bacteria. |
| "If I have a UTI, I will always have a fever" | A simple lower UTI (urethritis/cystitis) often causes NO fever. Fever signals the infection has moved up to the kidneys — a more serious condition. You can be very sick with a UTI without early fever. |
| "You can stop antibiotics once the burning stops" | Dangerous. Stopping early leaves the strongest bacteria behind, creating a resistant recurrent infection. Always finish the full prescribed course. |
