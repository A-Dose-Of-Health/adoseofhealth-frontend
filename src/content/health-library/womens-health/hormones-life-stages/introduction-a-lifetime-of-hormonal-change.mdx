## Introduction: A Lifetime of Hormonal Change

Your hormones are not static — they are a dynamic, living conversation between your brain and your body that changes across every stage of life. From the moment you are in your mother's womb, through puberty, your reproductive years and into menopause, these chemical messengers shape your health, your mood and your experience of being in the world. Hormonal changes are not something to fear — understanding them is the key to working with your body, not against it.

💡 **The HPG Axis — The Master Control System**

The **Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Gonadal (HPG) axis** is the hormonal command chain: the **hypothalamus** (brain) releases GnRH in pulses → the **pituitary gland** responds with FSH and LH → the **ovaries** produce estrogen and progesterone → these feed back to regulate the brain. This sensitive system responds to stress, illness, sleep changes and exercise — which is why your cycle can change during difficult times.

---

## The 6 Key Hormones

### Estrogen (Estradiol)

**Source:** Primarily ovaries; also fat tissue and adrenal glands.

**Role:** Develops secondary sex characteristics at puberty; thickens uterine lining; maintains bone density; supports skin health, mood and cardiovascular function; dominant in the first half of the cycle (follicular phase).

**Levels:** Peak at ovulation; decline sharply at menopause.

---

### Progesterone

**Source:** Corpus luteum after ovulation; placenta during pregnancy.

**Role:** Prepares the uterine lining for implantation; maintains early pregnancy; dominant in the second half of the cycle (luteal phase).

**Levels:** Rise sharply after ovulation; if no pregnancy, collapse triggers menstruation; decline erratically in perimenopause.

---

### Testosterone

**Source:** Ovaries and adrenal glands — yes, women produce testosterone naturally, just in smaller amounts than men.

**Role:** Libido, mood, energy, muscle mass, bone health.

**Note:** In women with PCOS, testosterone levels are often elevated, causing acne, facial hair growth and irregular ovulation.

---

### FSH — Follicle-Stimulating Hormone

**Source:** Pituitary gland (in the brain).

**Role:** Stimulates ovarian follicles to grow and mature at the start of each cycle. If no eggs remain (menopause), FSH rises dramatically as the pituitary tries harder and harder to stimulate ovaries that can no longer respond — elevated FSH is a marker of declining ovarian reserve.

---

### LH — Luteinising Hormone

**Source:** Pituitary gland.

**Role:** The mid-cycle **LH surge** (a sudden spike) triggers the dominant follicle to release a mature egg — this is ovulation. Ovulation predictor kits detect this surge. After ovulation, LH stimulates the ruptured follicle to become the corpus luteum, which then produces progesterone.

---

### Prolactin

**Source:** Pituitary gland.

**Role:** Primarily stimulates milk production after childbirth. High prolactin suppresses ovulation (which is why exclusive breastfeeding can temporarily prevent periods — the basis of LAM contraception).

**Note:** Abnormally elevated prolactin outside of breastfeeding can cause irregular or absent periods and should be investigated.

## Hormones Across All 5 Life Stages

### Stage 1: In the Beginning — Fetal Development & Infancy

**In the Womb**

The absence of male sex hormones allows the female reproductive tract to develop. By the **fifth month of gestation**, a female fetus already has her **lifetime supply of eggs** — about 6–7 million, already formed in her ovaries. This number declines naturally throughout life. You are born with all the eggs you will ever have — a fixed, non-renewable resource.

**"Minipuberty" — First Few Months of Life**

Shortly after birth, there is a temporary activation of the HPG axis. This "minipuberty" helps solidify the development of the ovaries and external genitalia. It happens behind the scenes — no outward signs — but is a critical developmental window. From birth until puberty, the HPG axis is then relatively quiet: a period of "rest" before adolescence.

---

### Stage 2: Puberty & Adolescence (Ages 8–19)

**What Triggers Puberty**

The hypothalamus begins releasing **GnRH in pulses**, stimulating the pituitary to release FSH and LH. These signal the ovaries to produce estrogen. The adrenal glands also begin producing androgens (DHEA) years before puberty in a process called **adrenarche** — which triggers pubic and underarm hair growth independent of the ovaries.

**Early vs Late Puberty Concerns**

- **Early puberty:** Signs before age 8 should be evaluated — can indicate precocious puberty.
- **Delayed puberty:** No breast development by age 13 requires evaluation.
- **Absent period:** No period by age 15 (primary amenorrhea) requires evaluation.
- **Irregular cycles:** Can take up to 5 years to regularise after menarche. By age 16, cycles should generally be predictable.

✨ **Good to Know — Earlier Onset Over Time**

The age of puberty onset has been decreasing over the last 150 years, largely due to improved health and nutrition. Factors like moderate excess body weight can lead to earlier puberty, and genetics (mother's age at menarche) play a role.

---

### Stage 3: The Reproductive Years (Ages 20–39)

**Peak Fertility & the Monthly Rhythm**

The HPG axis is fully mature and functioning in a classic cyclical pattern. A predictable ebb and flow of estrogen and progesterone drives the monthly cycle. **Peak fertility is in the 20s**. Fertility begins a gradual decline in the 30s, with a more noticeable drop after age 35. A healthy 30-year-old has about a **20% chance of conceiving in any given cycle**. By age 40, that drops to less than 5% per cycle.

**Egg Quality & Ovarian Reserve**

As a woman ages, so do her eggs. The risk of chromosomal abnormalities (like Down syndrome) and miscarriage increases because eggs are more likely to have abnormal chromosome numbers (aneuploidy). Tests like **AMH (anti-müllerian hormone)** and **day-3 FSH** can give an idea of ovarian reserve — but cannot perfectly predict the chance of pregnancy.

⚠️ **Fertility Guidance**

- Under 35 years: Seek help after **1 year** of regular, unprotected sex without conceiving
- Ages 35–40: Seek help after **6 months**
- Over 40 years: Seek help **immediately — do not wait**

---

### Stage 4: The Perimenopause Transition (Ages 40–Early 50s)

**The Hormonal Rollercoaster**

Contrary to popular belief, perimenopause is **not a steady decline** in hormones — it is characterised by **wild fluctuations**. Estrogen levels may swing from very high to very low unpredictably. Progesterone production becomes erratic as ovulation becomes less consistent. This volatility, not just low estrogen, causes many classic symptoms. The transition typically lasts **2–8 years**, beginning in the 40s.

**Cycle Changes**

The first sign is often a **shortening of the menstrual cycle** (cycles become 21–25 days apart). Later, cycles become longer and more irregular, with skipped periods. As the pool of ovarian follicles dwindles, the restraints on follicular growth (inhibin B and AMH) are lifted, leading to a final "burst" of follicular activity before the end.

| Symptom Category | What You Might Experience |
|-----------------|--------------------------| 
| **Vasomotor** | Hot flashes, night sweats (affect up to 80% of women). Caused by estrogen fluctuations affecting the brain's temperature regulation centre. |
| **Sleep** | Difficulty falling asleep and staying asleep — often linked to night sweats disrupting rest cycles |
| **Mood** | Irritability, mood swings, anxiety (can feel like severe PMS). A Feb 2026 review in *Biological Psychiatry* confirms estradiol fluctuations impact brain neurotransmitters (serotonin, dopamine, GABA), directly affecting mood. |
| **Cognitive** | Trouble concentrating, "brain fog," memory lapses — often temporary and related to sleep disruption and hormone fluctuations |
| **Physical** | Vaginal dryness (early sign), joint and muscle aches, headaches, skin changes, hair thinning |

💡 **You Can Still Get Pregnant During Perimenopause**

Ovulation is erratic but not impossible during perimenopause. Continue using contraception until you have gone **12 full months without a period**. Do not assume you cannot conceive just because your periods are irregular.

---

### Stage 5: Menopause & Beyond (Ages 50+)

**Definition & Timeline**

Menopause is defined as **12 consecutive months without a period**. Average age: **52 years** (typical range 46–55). With average life expectancy now over 65 in Kenya, most women will spend **over one-third of their lives** in the postmenopausal stage. Smoking can cause menopause about a year earlier.

**Long-Term Effects of Low Estrogen**

- **Bone:** Estrogen maintains bone density — its loss accelerates bone loss (osteoporosis risk).
- **Heart:** Estrogen protects the cardiovascular system — risk of heart disease and high cholesterol increases.
- **Genitourinary:** Vaginal tissues thin, dry and lose elasticity (GSM).
- **Brain:** Increased focus on cognitive health and dementia prevention strategies.

⚠️ **Any Bleeding After Menopause is NOT Normal**

Any bleeding after 12+ months without a period requires **urgent medical evaluation**. It could be a sign of endometrial (uterine) cancer or other serious conditions. Do not wait — even a single episode of spotting after menopause warrants immediate assessment.

---

## When to Seek Medical Help at Any Stage

- No period by age 15 (Primary Amenorrhea)
- Periods stop for 3+ months when not pregnant (Secondary Amenorrhea)
- Bleeding is extremely heavy — soaking a pad/tampon every hour
- Bleeding between periods or after sex
- Severe, debilitating pain that OTC medication does not help
- Sudden, severe pelvic pain (could be ectopic pregnancy or ovarian cyst rupture)
- Any bleeding after menopause — URGENT
- Severe mood symptoms that interfere with daily life (depression, anxiety, rage)
- Fever with pelvic pain
