# Menopause & Perimenopause

## Introduction: A Natural Transition, A New Understanding

Menopause is not a disease or a deficiency — it is a natural biological transition marking the end of the reproductive years. Yet for decades it has been shrouded in silence, stigma and outdated information. That is now changing in a landmark way. The median age for menopause is 52, with most women experiencing it between 45 and 55. With increasing life expectancy, women now spend approximately one-third of their lives in the postmenopausal stage — making understanding this transition more important than ever.

🚨 **Breaking News · November 2025**

**FDA Approves Historic Hormone Therapy Label Changes**

In November 2025, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved sweeping changes to hormone therapy labels — **removing decades-old "black box" warnings** that had frightened millions of women away from treatment since 2002. This is the most significant update in menopause medicine in over 20 years. These changes reflect updated science and the "timing hypothesis." See Part 4 below for full details.

---

## Part 1: Perimenopause — The Years Before

*Perimenopause, meaning "around menopause," is the transitional period leading up to menopause. It typically begins in the 40s but can start as early as the mid-30s. It can last 4–8 years. Contrary to popular belief, it is **not a steady decline** — it is characterised by wild hormonal fluctuations.*

### What Happens Hormonally

- **Estrogen levels swing wildly** — from very high to very low unpredictably
- **Progesterone production becomes erratic** as ovulation becomes less consistent
- Cycles may shorten first (21–25 days) then lengthen and become irregular with skipped periods
- The pool of ovarian follicles nears exhaustion — as they dwindle, restraints on follicular growth (inhibin B and AMH) are lifted, leading to a final "burst" of follicular activity before the end
- **This volatility** — not just low estrogen — causes many classic symptoms

---

### New Research: Estrogen & Depression Risk

A groundbreaking **February 2026 review in Biological Psychiatry** reveals that it is the *fluctuations* in estradiol (the most neuroactive form of estrogen) — not just low levels — that may trigger depression risk in vulnerable women during perimenopause.

Mechanisms identified:
- Neurotransmitter signalling — estrogen affects serotonin, dopamine and GABA
- Neuroimmune function — inflammation may play a role
- Brain glucose metabolism — energy supply to brain cells
- Synaptogenesis — formation of connections between neurons

💡 **You Can Still Get Pregnant During Perimenopause**

Ovulation is erratic but not impossible. Continue using contraception until you have gone **12 full consecutive months without a period**. The average age of menopause is 52 — factors like smoking can bring it about a year earlier.

## Part 2: Menopause — The Milestone

### Official Definition

Menopause is officially defined as the point when you have gone **12 consecutive months without a period**. At this point, the ovaries have largely ceased producing estrogen and progesterone, and ovulation no longer occurs.

**Average age:** 52 years (range: typically 46–55)  
**Premature menopause (&lt;40):** Called Primary Ovarian Insufficiency — requires evaluation and usually hormone therapy

---

### STRAW+10 Staging System

The gold standard for defining reproductive stages:

- **Reproductive:** Regular cycles, fertile (menarche to 40s)
- **Early perimenopause:** Variable cycles, ≤7 days difference from normal (40s)
- **Late perimenopause:** ≥60 days of no periods, fluctuating hormones (late 40s–early 50s)
- **Early postmenopause:** First 5–8 years after final period (50s)
- **Late postmenopause:** Beyond 8 years after final period (60+)

✨ **Induced Menopause**

Some women experience **induced menopause** from surgery (hysterectomy with ovary removal), chemotherapy or radiation. This sudden drop in hormones can cause more severe and sudden symptoms than natural menopause, as the body has no time to gradually adjust. Women experiencing induced menopause before 45 are particularly vulnerable to long-term health effects and should discuss hormone therapy with their provider.

## Part 3: Symptoms — What to Expect

*Up to 75% of women experience hot flashes · 20% report severe symptoms*

---

### I. Vasomotor Symptoms — Hot Flashes & Night Sweats

| Feature | Description |
|---------|------------|
| **What it feels like** | Sudden sensation of intense heat, often in the face, neck and chest; may cause flushing, sweating and skin reddening |
| **Duration** | Usually 1–5 minutes, but can last longer; frequency varies from a few per week to many per day |
| **Common triggers** | Hot drinks, spicy food, alcohol, stress, warm environments, tight clothing |
| **Night sweats** | Hot flashes during sleep — drench pyjamas and sheets; disrupt sleep cycles causing daytime fatigue and mood disturbance |
| **Duration of symptoms** | On average, vasomotor symptoms last 7–10 years. For about 10–15% of women, they persist indefinitely. Research shows Black women often experience more severe and longer-lasting vasomotor symptoms (SWAN study). |

---

### II. Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause (GSM)

This term encompasses vaginal and urinary symptoms caused by estrogen loss — affecting up to 45% of postmenopausal women. Unlike hot flashes which often lessen over time, **GSM is progressive without treatment**. Many women are too embarrassed to discuss these symptoms — but effective treatments exist.

**Vaginal Symptoms**

- Vaginal dryness, itching and burning
- Pain during sex (dyspareunia)
- Increased susceptibility to vaginal infections
- Decreased natural lubrication during arousal

*This does not mean your sex life is over — effective treatments exist. You should not "just tolerate" painful sex.*

**Urinary Symptoms**

- Urinary urgency (sudden strong need to go)
- Urinary frequency (needing to go often)
- Recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs)
- Urinary leakage with urgency (urge incontinence)

*Low-dose vaginal estrogen can dramatically improve all these symptoms — with minimal systemic absorption.*

---

### III. Other Common Symptoms

| Category | Symptoms |
|----------|---------|
| **Sleep** | Insomnia, night sweats, difficulty staying asleep — one of the most distressing symptoms for many women. Sleep deprivation worsens mood, cognition and physical symptoms. |
| **Mood** | Irritability, mood swings, anxiety, increased depression risk (see February 2026 estradiol research). Can feel like severe PMS. Does not mean you are "going mad." |
| **Cognitive** | "Brain fog" — difficulty concentrating, memory lapses, word-finding difficulties. Often temporary and related to sleep disruption and hormone fluctuations. Usually improves with adequate sleep and treatment. |
| **Physical** | Joint aches and muscle pain (many women are surprised by this); headaches; weight gain — especially around the middle; dry skin; thinning hair; brittle nails |

⚠️ **Not Everything Is Menopause**

Not every symptom in your 40s and 50s is caused by menopause. Thyroid disorders, vitamin deficiencies, depression, anaemia and other conditions can mimic menopause symptoms. Always get a thorough evaluation — do not assume everything is "just the menopause" without investigation.

## Part 4: The 2025 FDA Landmark Changes

*The Most Significant Update in Menopause Medicine in 20+ Years*

| Before November 2025 | After November 2025 |
|---------------------|---------------------|
| Black box warnings for cardiovascular disease, breast cancer and probable dementia on all HRT products | These warnings **removed** from prescribing information — replaced with updated, nuanced guidance |
| Strong warnings based entirely on the 2002 Women's Health Initiative (WHI) study | Updated warnings now reflect current science and the **"timing hypothesis"** |
| Millions of women and doctors avoided HRT even when clearly appropriate and beneficial | More women can now consider HRT as a safe option — estimated only 2 million of 41 million eligible US women received a prescription in 2020 |

---

### Why Did This Change Happen?

**The Study's Critical Flaw**

The WHI studied mostly **older women** (average age 63 — many a decade past menopause onset). The risks observed in this older group do not apply to women who start HRT within 10 years of menopause onset.

**The Timing Hypothesis**

Women who start HRT **within 10 years of menopause** or **before age 60** have a very different (more favourable) risk profile than women starting later. For this group, cardiovascular benefits may actually exist.

**What Remains**

The warning for **endometrial cancer** remains on systemic estrogen-alone therapies. Women with a uterus must still take **progestogen alongside estrogen** to protect the uterine lining. Individual risk assessment remains essential.

🇰🇪 **Kenya Context**

The FDA changes apply to US-approved hormone therapy products. In Kenya, the Pharmacy and Poisons Board may take time to update local labelling. However, the **scientific consensus has shifted globally** — Kenyan providers can and should prescribe based on current evidence. If you have avoided HRT due to fear of the "old" warnings, now is the time for a fresh conversation with your healthcare provider about your personal risk.

## Part 5: Treatment Options — What's Available Now

### 1. Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT / MHT)

The most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms and genitourinary syndrome of menopause. Involves taking estrogen (and progestogen for those with a uterus) to replace what the ovaries no longer make.

| Type | What It Is | For Whom |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Estrogen-only therapy** | Estrogen alone (systemic) | Women who have had a hysterectomy — no risk of endometrial cancer without a uterus |
| **Combined therapy (E+P)** | Estrogen + progestogen — the progestogen protects the uterine lining | Women with an intact uterus — essential to prevent endometrial cancer |
| **Low-dose vaginal estrogen** | Creams, tablets, rings applied or inserted vaginally | Women with only vaginal/urinary symptoms — minimal systemic absorption; extremely safe even in most breast cancer survivors |

---

### Routes of Administration

| Route | Forms | Key Advantage |
|-------|-------|--------------|
| **Oral (tablets)** | Pills taken daily | Convenient, widely available — but passes through the liver (higher blood clot risk than transdermal) |
| **Transdermal (through skin)** | Patches, gels, sprays applied to skin | Avoids first-pass liver metabolism — **lower blood clot and stroke risk** than oral HRT; preferred for women with clot risk |
| **Subcutaneous implant/pellet** | Small pellet inserted under skin of abdomen or buttock — lasts 4–8 months (may continue releasing up to 18–24 months) | Consistent hormone levels without daily attention; useful when other methods are ineffective |
| **Vaginal (local only)** | Creams, tablets, rings | Local effect only — minimal systemic absorption; recommended specifically for GSM symptoms |

---

### Benefits vs Risks of HRT

**Benefits**

- Most effective relief for hot flashes and night sweats
- Prevents bone loss and fractures (osteoporosis)
- Improves vaginal health and sexual function
- Improves sleep and mood
- When started within 10 years of menopause, may reduce all-cause mortality
- A Jan 2026 study in *Life Sciences* shows estrogen protects the heart, aorta and kidneys by activating cellular "cleaning" (autophagy) through estrogen receptors

**Risks (Context-Dependent)**

- Small increased risk of **breast cancer** with combined (E+P) therapy — duration-dependent; less with estrogen-only
- Small increased risk of **ovarian cancer** (becomes apparent within 5 years; diminishes after stopping)
- **Oral HRT** increases blood clot/pulmonary embolism risk — *transdermal does not*
- HRT does **not** prevent heart disease or cognitive decline and should not be prescribed solely for these purposes
- Individual risk assessment is always essential

---

### 2. Non-Hormonal Prescription Medications

| Medication | How It Works | Notes |
|-----------|------------|-------|
| **Elinzanatant (Lynkuet)** *Approved November 2025* | Blocks Neurokinin 1 (NK1) and Neurokinin 3 (NK3) receptors in the brain that regulate body temperature. A completely new non-hormonal mechanism of action. | For women who cannot or prefer not to take hormones. Side effects: fatigue, dizziness, drowsiness. May actually *improve* sleep as a side effect. |
| **SSRIs/SNRIs (antidepressants)** e.g., paroxetine, venlafaxine | Reduce frequency and severity of hot flashes — off-label use | Also beneficial for perimenopausal depression. Not all SSRIs are equally effective for hot flashes. |
| **Gabapentin** | Reduces hot flash frequency — off-label use | Particularly useful for night sweats; can cause dizziness and drowsiness |
| **Ospemifene (SERM)** | A selective estrogen receptor modulator for vaginal dryness and painful sex — taken as a daily tablet | An option for women who cannot use vaginal estrogen |
| **Vaginal moisturisers/lubricants** | Maintain vaginal moisture and reduce friction during sex | First-line treatment for GSM. Non-hormonal — can be used by anyone. Hyaluronic acid gels show comparable efficacy to vaginal estrogen in some studies. |

---

### 3. Lifestyle Approaches

**Cooling Strategies & Triggers**

- Dress in layers to easily remove when hot
- Use fans or cool water on your face and neck
- Keep your bedroom cool for sleep
- **Common triggers to avoid:** hot drinks, spicy food, alcohol, caffeine, stress, warm environments

**Exercise, Diet & Mind-Body**

- **Regular physical activity** — improves mood, sleep, bone health and cardiovascular health
- **Diet:** Work with a dietitian; moderate caffeine and alcohol; phytoestrogens (soy, flaxseed) — mixed evidence but generally safe
- **Stress management:** Yoga, meditation, deep breathing, CBT
- **Pelvic floor exercises (Kegels):** Help with urinary symptoms and vaginal tone

## Part 6: Long-Term Health After Menopause

*With lower estrogen levels, certain health risks increase after menopause. Being proactive — starting lifestyle changes well before menopause — can make a significant difference to long-term outcomes. Start building bone and heart health NOW, at whatever age you are reading this.*

| Health Area | What Changes | What to Do |
|------------|------------|-----------|
| **🦴 Bone Health** | Estrogen maintains bone density. After menopause, bone loss accelerates — increasing risk of **osteoporosis** and fractures (especially hip, wrist, shoulder). The first 5 years after menopause see the fastest bone loss. | Adequate calcium intake; Vitamin D (sunlight + supplements); **weight-bearing exercise** (walking, jogging, dancing); **strength and resistance training**; bone density screening (DEXA scan); medications if needed (bisphosphonates, denosumab). Start these habits in your 20s and 30s. |
| **❤️ Heart Health** | Estrogen has a protective effect on the cardiovascular system. After menopause, risk of heart disease, high cholesterol and stroke increases. Heart disease is the leading cause of death in postmenopausal women. | Monitor blood pressure and cholesterol regularly; heart-healthy diet (low salt, healthy fats, lots of vegetables); regular aerobic exercise; do not smoke or vape; manage stress; control diabetes and blood pressure if present. |
| **🧠 Brain Health** | The February 2026 estradiol review highlights estrogen's importance for brain function. Brain fog during perimenopause is common — usually temporary. Long-term, managing vascular risk factors is key for cognitive health. | Stay mentally active (reading, learning, puzzles); social engagement; physical activity; manage blood pressure, diabetes and other chronic conditions. HRT is not recommended solely for cognitive protection. |
| **🌸 Pelvic Floor Health** | Years after menopause, pelvic floor issues may emerge: pelvic organ prolapse (bladder, uterus or bowel descending into vagina) and urinary incontinence become more common. | Pelvic floor exercises (Kegels) — do them daily, starting well before menopause; maintain healthy weight; treat constipation (straining worsens prolapse); seek pelvic floor physiotherapy if available; vaginal oestrogen helps maintain pelvic tissue health. |

## Myth-Busting: Menopause

| ❌ Myth | ✅ Truth |
|--------|---------|
| "Menopause means you are old and your life is over" | Menopause marks the end of fertility, not the end of vitality. Many women enter their most vibrant, productive decades after menopause. In Kenya, with average life expectancy now over 65, you may live one-third of your life after menopause — make it count. |
| "Menopause symptoms are just something women must endure" | While menopause is natural, severe symptoms are treatable. Effective options exist, and the 2025 FDA updates mean more women can now access hormone therapy safely without fear based on outdated warnings. |
| "Hormone therapy causes cancer, full stop" | The relationship is complex. Combined HRT slightly increases breast cancer risk with long-term use (5+ years), but the absolute risk is small. Estrogen-only therapy (for women without a uterus) shows little to no increased breast cancer risk. Individual risk assessment with your provider is essential. |
| "If I still have periods, I can't be in perimenopause" | Perimenopause is defined by *changing* periods, not their absence. Cycles may shorten, lengthen or become irregular while still occurring. Perimenopause typically lasts 4–8 years before periods stop completely. |
| "Herbal remedies are safer than hormones" | "Natural" does not always mean safe. Herbal remedies are not regulated like pharmaceuticals; they can have side effects and interact with other medications. Evidence for most herbal remedies for menopause is limited. Always tell your provider about any herbs you take. |
| "Menopause causes madness" | Mood changes are common due to hormonal fluctuations, but severe mental illness is not caused by menopause. Depression risk does increase during perimenopause — but this is treatable. Seek help, don't suffer in silence. |

## Part 7: Emerging Research — The Future of Menopause Care

### Estrogen Receptor-Autophagy Axis (Jan 2026)

A January 2026 study in *Life Sciences* reveals a previously unknown mechanism: **estrogen protects the heart, aorta and kidneys by activating autophagy** (cellular "cleaning") through estrogen receptors. This research opens new targets for therapy and confirms estrogen's organ-protective effects beyond just symptom relief.

---

### Regenerative Research — Experimental

Areas under active research (not yet available for routine use):

- Stem cell transplantation — regenerating ovarian tissue
- Intraovarian PRP (platelet-rich plasma) — rejuvenating ovarian function
- Ovarian tissue cryopreservation — preserving fertility or delaying menopause
- Telomerase activators — potentially slowing cellular ovarian aging
- Metabolic modulators (metformin, rapamycin) — under study for ovarian aging

💡 **The Goal**

These innovations aim not just to extend fertility but to **preserve endocrine health, enhance quality of life and promote healthy aging**. The field of "reproductive longevity" is emerging as a recognised area of medicine — pointing toward a future where the postmenopausal years can be actively managed for optimal health.

## When to Seek Medical Help

| Seek Medical Advice If: | Urgency Level |
|------------------------|--------------|
| Your symptoms are affecting your quality of life (hot flashes, sleep, mood) | Book an appointment — treatments exist |
| Heavy bleeding or bleeding after a long stretch of no periods | Promptly — requires investigation |
| You are under 40 and suspect perimenopause (possible Primary Ovarian Insufficiency) | Promptly — POI needs diagnosis and usually HRT |
| Severe mood symptoms (depression, anxiety, rage) affecting your life | Promptly — treatable; do not suffer |
| You want to discuss HRT or other treatment options | Anytime — you deserve to make informed choices |
| **Any bleeding after menopause — even once, even just spotting** | **URGENT — same day or emergency** |
| Sudden, severe pelvic pain | **URGENT — emergency care** |
| Heavy bleeding with dizziness or fainting | **URGENT — emergency care** |
